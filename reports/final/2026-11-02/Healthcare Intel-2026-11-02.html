1. Employer PBM contracts become auditable before the broader rebate pass-through mandate

What happened

The Labor Department finalized requirements for PBMs and affiliated brokers or consultants serving large self-insured ERISA plans, generally those with at least 100 participants or sponsored by employers with at least 100 employees. Covered firms must disclose direct compensation, manufacturer payments, spread compensation, pharmacy recoupments and affiliate-steering arrangements. Plans receive initial disclosures before contracting or renewal, ongoing disclosures semiannually or quarterly upon request, and rights to audit the information. The detailed requirements apply to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027. (U.S. Department of Labor, Pharmacy Benefit Manager Fee Disclosure Final Rule, October 29, 2026)

Implication

This converts PBM transparency from an RFP preference into an ERISA contracting control. Vertically integrated PBMs will need plan-level data lineage across rebates, pharmacies, aggregators and consulting affiliates; employers will need the analytics and expertise to test whether compensation is reasonable.

The near-term demand is for contract normalization, rebate accounting, audit support and fiduciary reporting. The margin effect is uncertain because PBMs can redesign fees, guarantees and network terms rather than surrender the disclosed economics.

Watch next

Whether employers use the data to change vendors or primarily renegotiate incumbent contracts, and how PBMs reconcile these disclosures with the separate 2028 Part D compensation regime.

2. Bon Secours buys national scale in outpatient surgery

What happened

HealthSpan Partners, an affiliate and supporting organization of Bon Secours Mercy Health, agreed to acquire Surgery Partners in an all-cash transaction valued at approximately $5.7 billion, including debt. Public shareholders will receive $23.50 per share, while Bain Capital will roll its ownership into the acquiring entity. Surgery Partners contributes more than 200 locations across 30 states, including ambulatory surgery centers, surgical hospitals and physician practices; closing is expected in the first quarter of 2027, subject to shareholder and regulatory approvals. (Surgery Partners and HealthSpan Partners, HealthSpan Partners to Acquire Surgery Partners, October 26, 2026)

Implication

Bon Secours is buying a national site-of-care platform rather than assembling regional ASCs one partnership at a time. The transaction adds physician alignment, procedure growth and lower-cost capacity while reducing dependence on inpatient hospital economics.

The competitive effect is greater concentration among health-system-backed ASC platforms, increasing payer leverage over site-of-care steering but also creating a scaled counterparty in network negotiations. Technology and services vendors should expect centralized procurement, platform standardization and possible displacement across RCM, scheduling, supply-chain and clinical systems.

Watch next

Change-of-control provisions in payer and physician agreements, regulatory conditions, and whether HealthSpan directs new development toward Bon Secours markets or preserves Surgery Partners’ national partnership model.

3. Medicaid frailty ruling creates a temporary two-speed work-requirement rollout

What happened

On October 29, a federal court vacated CMS’s requirement that people within Congress’s listed medical-frailty categories also demonstrate that their condition significantly impairs their ability to satisfy Medicaid work requirements. The court permanently enjoined the challenged provision and gave the plaintiff states a six-month enforcement stay for determinations of whether an individual qualifies as medically frail. It upheld CMS’s 12-month limit on claims and encounter data and restrictions on repeated self-attestation. The statutory January 1, 2027 implementation date remains unchanged. (U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts, Massachusetts v. Oz Memorandum and Order, October 29, 2026)

Implication

The ruling reduces near-term disenrollment risk for medically complex members, but only partially simplifies administration. Plaintiff states gain implementation relief while other states continue preparing for January determinations, creating different enrollment and acuity trajectories across Medicaid markets.

MCOs need state-specific forecasts rather than a national work-requirement assumption. Eligibility vendors must support rapidly configurable exemption logic, twelve-month evidence rules and defensible audit trails. Providers receive some protection against coverage loss among high-need patients, although verification friction remains.

Watch next

Whether HHS appeals or issues nationwide guidance, and how plaintiff states use the six-month stay while preserving eligibility-system readiness.

Plans undertaking claims-platform migrations need parallel-run controls, appeal analytics and prepayment and postpayment integrity capacity before processing volatility reaches reserving and guidance.

5. HTI-5 lowers certification costs while shifting AI governance to buyers

What happened

ONC finalized removal of 14 of 60 health-IT certification criteria and revisions to seven others, a narrower reduction than the 34 removals proposed. The rule eliminates federal “model card” source-attribute requirements for decision-support interventions while retaining 39 criteria, including the electronic prior-authorization and real-time prescription-benefit capabilities adopted under HTI-4. Most provisions take effect December 28, 2026, with specified provisions effective January 1, 2027. ONC estimates annualized savings of $108.1 million beginning in 2027. (Federal Register, HTI-5 Final Rule, October 28, 2026)

Implication

Established EHR vendors receive lower maintenance costs, while smaller developers face fewer certification barriers. The rule preserves federal momentum behind FHIR and prior authorization, so interoperability investment should shift toward APIs rather than contract.

Removing model-card requirements weakens the federal minimum for transparency into clinical AI. Health systems and plans will increasingly need procurement terms, local validation and monitoring requirements to replace that assurance, favoring vendors that can document model provenance and performance voluntarily.

Watch next

ONC’s treatment of the deferred information-blocking proposals and the next FHIR-based certification rule, which will determine whether deregulation broadens competition or mainly reduces incumbent compliance expense.

  • December 28, 2026: Most HTI-5 provisions become effective. (Federal Register, HTI-5 Final Rule, October 28, 2026)
  • January 1, 2027: Medicaid community-engagement requirements begin; the court’s six-month plaintiff-state enforcement stay does not alter the statutory implementation date. (U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts, Massachusetts v. Oz Memorandum and Order, October 29, 2026)

  • July 1, 2027: The detailed PBM disclosure rule applies to plan years beginning on or after this date. (U.S. Department of Labor, Pharmacy Benefit Manager Fee Disclosure Final Rule, October 29, 2026)

Bottom Line

The week’s most important change is the conversion of employer PBM transparency into an auditable ERISA contracting requirement. PBM compensation data and contract-renewal infrastructure need the closest attention, while HCA’s $400 million uninsured-volume impact shows that coverage continuity is already affecting provider earnings. The December 15 CMS comment deadline is the next event most likely to determine how similar controls reshape Part D economics.
