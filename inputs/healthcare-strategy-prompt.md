## Delta-first requirement

This is a weekly change report, not a recurring overview of U.S. healthcare. Review the supplied previous weekly briefings and treat them as the baseline of what the reader already knows.

The central question is:

> What did we learn this week that materially changes, strengthens, weakens, resolves, or extends our existing understanding of payer economics, provider economics, or the health-technology and services demand that sits on those economics?

Do not repeat a development, regulation, Stars or RAF setup, vendor strategy, company thesis, or strategic conclusion merely because it remains important. A reader who read last week's briefing should not encounter substantial passages that could have appeared essentially unchanged the week before. It is preferable to produce a shorter briefing during a quiet week than to fill space with old material.

## Inclusion test

A development should generally appear only if it qualifies as at least one of:

- **NEW:** A genuinely new development, data print, policy action, company disclosure, competitive move, outage, transaction, or strategic hypothesis emerged.
- **UPDATE:** New information materially changes the magnitude, timing, probability, economics, or strategic significance of something previously discussed.
- **CONFIRM:** Significant new evidence materially strengthens an existing hypothesis.
- **REFUTE:** New evidence materially weakens or contradicts an existing hypothesis.
- **RESOLVE:** New information answers an important question or catalyst identified previously.

Mere consistency with an existing thesis is generally insufficient. Before including something, ask:

> If I removed this item, would the reader lose consequential information that became known, materially changed, or became meaningfully more certain during the last seven days?

If not, omit it. Do not write stub sections for payers, providers, CMS, interoperability, companies, quality, or the calendar when nothing material moved.

## Analysis expected for each major development

Do not merely summarize articles. For each consequential development, work through:

**What happened -> Why it matters -> Strategist implication -> What to watch next**

Inside the implication, assess the first affected market or administrative process, the transmission to plan economics, provider cash/operations, or health-tech demand, the exposure affected, directional implications for payer margins, provider yield, and administrative/clinical-tech spend, likely magnitude, time horizon, and confidence. Distinguish confirmed facts, estimates, management opinions, advocacy, inference and scenarios. Rank developments by likely effect on those economics, not by headline volume.

Typical transmission paths, when they apply:

- MA rate, RAF, coding-intensity, or RADV change → plan bids, supplemental benefits, and chart-review / in-home-assessment / PI vendor demand
- Stars or HEDIS methodology change → quality-bonus revenue, care-gap programs, and quality-tech spend
- Medical-cost trend or utilization surprise → UM, PI, and specialty-management intensity
- Prior-auth, FHIR, TEFCA, or clearinghouse rule/outage → claims flow, denials, and EHR/clearinghouse/RCM switching
- Denial, underpayment, NSA/IDR, or audit action → provider cash conversion and RCM/PI wallets
- Labor, capacity, or site-of-care shift → provider margins and automation / ambulatory / home-care demand
- Vertical integration, PBM, or antitrust action → who captures administrative surplus

When relevant, identify whether the development creates a new hypothesis, strengthens an existing one, weakens or revises one, or resolves a previously identified uncertainty.

For continuing stories:

1. Briefly state the prior expectation or thesis.
2. Lead with the new information.
3. Explain how it changes the prior view.
4. Explain the incremental strategist implication.
5. Identify the next catalyst or evidence that matters.

Do not repeat the entire background narrative. Do not infer causation from equity-price movement alone, fabricate unavailable values, or overstate importance. State "reliable public value unavailable" rather than inventing a medical-cost trend, RAF coefficient, Stars cutoff, denial rate, improper-payment rate, IDR win rate, or vendor GTM figure.

## Desired output

Target approximately 1,000-1,500 words, but allow shorter reports during quiet weeks. Produce polished Markdown using this general structure when it improves clarity:

Number **4-6** interpretive headlines at the same Markdown level as `## Executive View` (`## 1. ...`, `## 2. ...`). Separate stories with a horizontal rule (`---`). Use stacked labeled paragraphs, not one dense paragraph of inline bold labels.

The exact headings may vary when a clearer narrative is available. Prioritize strategic insight over filling every section.

Do **not**:

- Number coverage topics (payers, PI, RAF, quality, claims, RCM, CMS, companies, calendar) as a 1-12 outline
- Nest the week's stories under a parent heading such as "Strategy Narrative" or "Major strategic developments"
- Emit a `# Strategy Narrative` or `## Strategy Narrative` heading; the report page already wraps this brief
- Require a quantitative scoreboard table. Fold dependable public levels into the relevant story. If a widely watched series has no reliable public value, say so in that story rather than building a mostly empty table
- Fill empty payer, provider, CMS, interoperability, company, or calendar chapters
- Cover drug pipelines, pivotal-trial readouts, FDA approvals, or device launches unless they directly change plan medical cost, provider mix, or administrative-tech demand this week

Citation standards: Cite and link all important factual, quantitative and regulatory claims immediately after the relevant claim. Prefer original documents. For every source identify publisher, title, publication date and link. Do not cite search-results pages or sources that do not support the claim. Keep quotations brief and paraphrase wherever possible.

Editorial standards: Analytical, restrained and commercially useful. Avoid sensationalism, generic commentary, repetition, unsupported certainty, immaterial stories, stock recommendations, personalized trading instructions, treating management commentary as fact, and implying correlation proves causation.

Research standards: Scan the named primary-source circuit every week even if most items produce nothing to write. Use web search as many times as needed to cover the reporting window, retrieve original CMS/OIG/SEC documents, reconcile contradictions, and verify dates. Breadth belongs in research; brevity belongs in the brief.
