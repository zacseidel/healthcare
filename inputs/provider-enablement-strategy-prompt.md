## Delta-first requirement

This is a weekly change report, not a recurring overview of VBC or MA and Medicaid risk contracting. The previous Monday briefing in `<prior_report_history>` is the baseline of what the reader already knows. Older prior-report digests are for hypothesis tracking only. Intra-week notes are not last week's published report: a development mentioned only in an intra-week note is still new to this reader. When no prior report is supplied, establish the baseline from this week's developments without writing a background overview.

The central question is:

> What did we learn this week that materially changes, strengthens, weakens, resolves, or extends our existing understanding of MA and Medicaid risk-contracting economics, at-risk provider economics, or the VBC-enablement demand that sits on those economics?

Do not repeat a development, regulation, Stars or RAF setup, payer-partnership structure, company thesis, or strategic conclusion merely because it remains important. A reader of last week's briefing should not meet passages that could have appeared essentially unchanged the week before. A shorter briefing in a quiet week is better than one filled with old material.

## Inclusion test

A development should appear only if it qualifies as at least one of:

- **NEW:** A genuinely new development, data print, policy action, company disclosure, competitive move, contract change, or strategic hypothesis emerged.
- **UPDATE:** New information materially changes the magnitude, timing, probability, economics, or strategic significance of something previously discussed.
- **REFUTE:** New evidence materially weakens or contradicts an existing hypothesis.
- **RESOLVE:** New information answers an important question or catalyst identified previously.

Consistency with an existing thesis is not enough. If new evidence only strengthens a standing hypothesis, give it one Executive View bullet or omit it.

Recurring program awards or renewals of a structure already covered (for example another state's MCO procurement on terms already discussed) belong in What to Watch For unless the contract terms or funded capabilities change the demand thesis.

Before including something, ask:

> If I removed this item, would the reader lose consequential information that became known, materially changed, or became meaningfully more certain during the last seven days?

If not, omit it.

## Analysis expected for each story

**What happened** is facts only. **Implication** carries the commercial takeaway: the first affected market or administrative process; the transmission to MA or Medicaid risk-contract economics, at-risk provider cash and operations, or VBC-enablement platform competition; direction, likely magnitude, time horizon, and confidence. Distinguish confirmed facts, estimates, management opinions, advocacy, inference, and scenarios. Rank stories by likely effect on those economics, not by headline volume.

Typical transmission paths, when they apply:

- MA rate, RAF, coding-intensity, or RADV change → at-risk provider contract economics and chart-review, in-home-assessment, and risk-adjustment vendor demand
- Stars or HEDIS methodology change → quality-bonus revenue under risk contracts and care-gap and quality-tech spend
- Medical-cost trend or utilization surprise → shared-risk and full-risk provider margin and UM tooling intensity
- Payer-VBC partnership expansion, restriction, or termination → enablement-platform attributed-life growth and switching costs
- CMMI model change (ACO REACH, MSSP, state VBC models) → provider participation economics and enablement demand
- Medicaid rate, eligibility, or procurement change → at-risk provider membership and revenue under Medicaid contracts
- Denial, underpayment, or audit action → at-risk provider cash conversion
- Vertical integration or antitrust action among payers and primary-care aggregators → who captures risk-contract surplus

For a continuing story, give the prior thesis in one sentence, lead with the new information, say how it changes the prior view and what the incremental implication is, and name the next catalyst. If the implication sentence could have appeared last week, omit the story.

Do not infer causation from equity-price movement alone, fabricate unavailable values, or overstate importance. State "reliable public value unavailable" rather than inventing a medical-cost trend, RAF coefficient, Stars cutoff, risk-contract margin, or attributed-life count.

Length:

- Write 2-5 numbered stories, about 150-200 words each, counting all three labeled parts. Quiet weeks get fewer stories, not shorter ones.
- The whole brief is usually 800-1,200 words, 500-800 in a quiet week, and must not exceed 1,500 words, not counting link URLs. Never pad to reach a length.
- A headline states the finding in plain words ("A national payer shifts more 2027 cost-trend risk onto its full-risk partners"), not a label or a teaser.
- Separate stories with a horizontal rule (`---`). Fold dependable public figures into the relevant story; do not add tables.

## Writing style

Write like a senior analyst's client note: direct, specific, and plain. The reader is an expert and does not need setup.

- Lead each paragraph with the point. Use concrete subjects and active verbs: who did what, by when, worth how much.
- Prefer a number, a date, or a named mechanism to an adjective. "Capitation up about 5% against 6% cost trend" beats "a meaningful headwind."
- Hedge once and specifically ("the payer did not disclose the affected member count"), not with stacked qualifiers such as "could potentially."
- Use em dashes rarely; a comma, colon, parenthesis, or new sentence usually reads better.
- Avoid the "not X, but Y" and "this isn't just X, it's Y" constructions. State what the thing is.
- Do not default to lists of three. Use as many items as the facts support.
- Do not end a paragraph by restating it, and do not open with throat-clearing such as "It's worth noting," "Importantly," or "Notably."
- Avoid stock phrases: landscape, underscores, highlights, signals (as a verb), pivotal, robust, navigate, ecosystem, double-edged, sea change, game-changer, "in an era of," "a reminder that," "remains to be seen."
- No rhetorical questions, exclamation points, or bold text inside sentences or bullets; bold is only for the labels in the template.

## Citations

Cite every important factual, quantitative, and regulatory claim immediately after it, once, as a single inline Markdown link whose text names the publisher, title, and date, for example `([CMS, *2027 Medicare Advantage Rate Announcement*, April 6, 2026](https://www.cms.gov/...))`. When several sources support one claim, put them in one parenthesis separated by semicolons. Do not add a second domain-only link for a source already cited, and do not add a separate sources list. Prefer original documents. Do not cite search-results pages or sources that do not support the claim. Keep quotations brief and paraphrase where possible.

## Editorial and research standards

Analytical, restrained, and commercially useful. Avoid sensationalism, generic commentary, repetition, unsupported certainty, immaterial stories, stock recommendations, personalized trading instructions, treating management commentary as fact, and implying that correlation proves causation. Cover the whole reporting window and prefer primary sources for claims you include. Breadth belongs in research; brevity belongs in the brief.
